Which pages should a content team review first?

Hassan Ali Akhund · Applied Search Intelligence track · 2026 · Code and notebooks

Abstract

Content teams can review a few dozen pages a month out of tens of thousands that still earn search demand, so the useful question is which pages to look at first: the ones about to lose visibility faster than the rest of their own site. Using FlyRank's pseudonymized search warehouse, I built page-level decisions across four months (March to June 2026), each with features from one month and an outcome measured in the next. I compared a transparent rule (sliding impressions plus below-par click-through for the page's position) against a random forest, scoring both on the same pages under client-grouped, time-forward and sealed-month validation. The forest was measured ahead on full-list ranking in every honest test (sealed June ROC AUC against ), while the two were roughly level for the top 50 to 500 pages a team can actually review. The output is a ranked July review queue with an action and reason codes per page, offered as decision support; it does not show that a refresh causes recovery.

1. Problem

Picture the first Monday of the month. An editor has time to open about fifty pages. Their accounts have more than ninety thousand pages with real search demand, and a quarter of those will lose visibility next month faster than the rest of their own site. Which fifty?

A wrong pick wastes an hour of review. A missed pick costs whatever clicks the page keeps losing until someone notices, and that cost grows with how much demand the page had. So this work is judged where the decision happens: precision at the top of the list (how many of the first K pages really declined) and the share of lost demand those K pages account for. Accuracy across every page would reward getting page 60,000 right, and nobody ever opens page 60,000.

The obvious rule, "big pages that are slipping", fails on the first check: in this data bigger pages decline less often. That tension is where the work starts.

2. Data

The data is FlyRank's internship warehouse release on Hugging Face (build v20260703, exported 3 July 2026). Every client and page id in it is a salted pseudonym. No names, domains, URLs, titles or search queries exist in the release, and none appear here.

I used the daily performance fact table (one row per day, client and page), monthly partitions from February to June 2026, and the content dimension table for each page's creation date. Features come from month M and the outcome from month M+1, never sharing a day. Search Console rows exist only on days a page was shown, so a client counts as tracked only if its pages have rows on every day of both months; a page with no rows at a tracked client earned zero impressions. Pages qualify with at least 100 impressions in the feature month and at least 30 days of age.

3. Methodology

Label

A page is labeled declined when its impressions per day in the outcome month fall more than 20% below the feature month and that change is at least 20 points worse than the median change across its own client's pages. The second condition matters: in a single month, client-level medians ranged from about -87% to +96%. A page that falls with its whole site needs a site-level look, not a page refresh.

Features

Seven, all computed from the feature month or from the fixed creation date: log impressions, days visible, impression-weighted average position, late-month momentum (log impressions per day in the second half of the month minus the first half), content age, click-through rate, and the CTR gap against the median CTR of the page's position tier (top 3, 4 to 10, 11 to 20, 21 to 50, 50+), with tier medians fitted on training rows only.

Signals checked before the rule

Each candidate signal was tested against the outcome in April before it could enter a rule. Two held, one went the wrong way.

Baseline and model

The baseline is a rule frozen in week 4: flag a page when momentum is below -0.1 and its CTR is below its tier's median, then rank flagged pages by how hard they slid. Everything else scores zero. The model is a random forest (300 trees, leaves of at least 50 pages), picked over logistic regression and gradient boosting because it ranked the full list best and boosting added nothing.

Validation and leakage checks

Both methods are scored on exactly the same pages in every design: a random 80/20 page split (the "before"), five-fold cross-validation grouped by client, time-forward (train on the March and April decisions, test on May), and a sealed June month scored once with the final configuration. The checks run as code assertions: feature and outcome windows never overlap, no label-derived or snapshot field is a feature, and no product flag is present. Deliberately adding one label-derived column lifted held-out AUC from 0.66 to 0.89, which confirmed the harness catches a leak. A client-level momentum feature was dropped after it helped only where clients repeat between training and test.

Two error patterns repeat in every month. Pages ranked high that didn't decline mostly dipped late in the month and then recovered, or fell almost exactly as much as their own site. The costliest misses are very large pages, visible every day and even rising, that collapsed the next month with no warning. Most declines (about 87% in June) sit outside the forest's top 5,000, beyond any review queue.

5. Limitations and honest framing

What this supports: on four monthly decisions from one pseudonymized release, a seven-feature random forest was measured ahead of a transparent rule on full-list ranking quality under client-grouped, time-forward and sealed-month validation, and the two were roughly level for the pages a team can review. That's observed, directional, decision-support evidence.

What it doesn't support:

  • No causal claim. The data shows which pages declined, not whether a refresh would have stopped it. That needs an experiment: refresh a random half of the queue and compare.
  • Nothing about Google's ranking systems. These are associations in one portfolio of 24 to 41 tracked clients a month.
  • The label is a choice. "More than 20% down and 20 points worse than the client" is defensible, not given. Other thresholds move the base rate and every number here.
  • One month of features. Sudden collapses of large, steady pages are invisible to a one-month window.
  • Client concentration. One client can dominate a month, so per-client caps and site checks are part of using the queue.
  • Staleness can't be measured at decision time in this release, so the signal behind the classic refresh flag isn't tested.

6. Ranked recommendations

The final model, retrained on all four labeled months, scores pages from clients for the 1 July 2026 decision using their June data. The queue is ordered by demand at risk (decline probability times impressions per day), with at most 10 pages per client in the top 50. July has no outcome in the release, so this is the queue a team would receive, not a result.

  1. Run indexing checks first. Pages that dropped out of search late in the month ( of the top 50) almost always stayed down in every labeled month. Checking for noindex, 404s, redirects or merges takes minutes; do it before any content work.
  2. Refresh and rewrite the sliding, under-clicked pages. of the top 50 are losing impressions and earning fewer clicks than their position usually gets: update the content, then rewrite the title and meta description.
  3. Refresh sliding pages whose snippets are fine. more: update the content and leave the snippet alone.
  4. Rewrite snippets on under-clicked page-one pages that aren't sliding. Low effort, and the position is already there.
  5. When one client hits the 10-page cap, check the site before the pages. Ten pages sliding at once on one site is more likely a site event than ten content problems.
  6. Log every action and re-score next month. Next month's outcome becomes the next label. Leaving a random half of the queue untouched is how a team would learn whether the refreshes themselves help.

7. Reproducibility

Everything on this page is generated by one notebook, work/notebooks/capstone.ipynb, which writes the data this page reads (paper/results.json). The weekly notebooks show each step on its own: data contract, baseline, model, and validation audit. The shared loader and frame builder is work/scripts/warehouse.py.

To rerun: clone the repo, install pinned dependencies from assignment-06/requirements.txt, accept the dataset's terms on Hugging Face, set a read token as HF_TOKEN, and run the notebook top to bottom. The random seed is 42 everywhere; the February to June files (about 640 MB) download once and are cached.
